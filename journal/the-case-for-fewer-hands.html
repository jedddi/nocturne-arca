The case for fewer hands.

Why our bench will never scale — and why that is precisely the point.

18 May MMXXVI · 6 minutes · Maison Nocturne

There is a number, rarely written down, that decides the character of almost every object you own: how many hands touched it before it reached yours. A phone passes through hundreds. A luxury watch, fewer — but more than you would guess. We have spent a great deal of effort keeping that number small, and we are often asked why. The honest answer is that each additional hand is also an additional opinion, an additional deadline, and an additional chance to settle.

Scale is usually treated as a virtue. More units, more makers, more reach. But scale has a cost that does not appear on a balance sheet: it converts judgement into procedure. When one person finishes a case, they decide when it is done. When forty people finish cases, "done" has to be written into a specification so that all forty agree — and the specification, by necessity, describes the acceptable, not the uncompromised.

Our bench holds a handful of people. A watchmaker. An optical technician. A finisher. Each instrument passes through the same hands every time, and those hands remember the last one. That memory is not sentimentality — it is the mechanism by which a piece gets better instead of merely consistent.

An instrument leaves the bench when its maker is unwilling to improve it further — not a day sooner, and never to meet a number.

The arithmetic of compromise

Consider a finish. A single maker brushing a titanium case can chase a flaw for an extra twenty minutes because no one is waiting on the next operation. Multiply that maker by forty and the twenty minutes becomes a bottleneck; the flaw becomes "within tolerance"; the tolerance becomes the product. Nothing dramatic happens. The watch is still good. It is simply no longer the best that hands could have made — and the difference, while small, is exactly the difference we exist to protect.

This is why our editions are numbered to the hundreds rather than the thousands. The limit is not a marketing device; it is a consequence. A small bench can only finish so many pieces before it would have to grow — and growing is the one thing we have decided not to do. When an edition closes, it closes because the hands that made it have moved on to the next idea.

There is a quieter benefit, too. The same people who finish a piece are the ones who service it years later. A repair is not handed to a stranger reading a manual; it returns to the bench that knew it. An object made by few hands can also be kept by few hands — and a thing that can be kept does not need to be replaced.

So we will not scale, and we will say so plainly. Fewer hands is not a limitation we tolerate. It is the instrument's first specification, and the last one we would ever remove.
